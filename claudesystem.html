What branding is and isn't.

Branding is not a logo. A logo is a signature — useful for confirming who wrote something, useless for making anyone want to read it. Companies that treat the logo as the brand end up with a beautiful mark sitting on top of work that looks like everyone else's.

Branding is also not taste. It is not whether the founder likes blue. A brand decision is right when it makes the company easier to recognise and harder to confuse with a competitor, and that is a question with an answer rather than an opinion.

What branding actually is: the set of repeated decisions that make your output identifiable before anyone reads the name on it. Type, spacing, how photographs are treated, how numbers are presented, the rhythm of the writing.

In B2B, the practical test is volume. You will publish hundreds of things this year — posts, decks, event graphics, one-pagers, reports. If each one is designed from scratch, most of them will not get made, and the ones that do will not look related.

So the system has to be usable by people who are not designers. That means fewer options, not more: a fixed set of layouts, a decided way to crop an image, a template for a claim. Constraints are what make speed possible.

And it has to be specific enough to be worth having. A system built from defaults produces work indistinguishable from a competitor using the same defaults — which is the problem we started with, arrived at more efficiently.

FAQ

What is a brand system?

A small set of decisions made once — type, colour, grid, image treatment, how a claim is worded, what a chart looks like — so that every piece made afterwards is a variation rather than a fresh invention. It is not a logo and a colour palette in a PDF nobody opens. It is the thing that lets someone who is not a designer produce something on brand on a Tuesday afternoon.

Why does B2B need one more than B2C?

Because B2B publishes constantly and rarely has a design team. Six to ten people at the buyer each need to encounter you several times before they agree, which means volume — posts, decks, one-pagers, event graphics, reports. Without a system, that volume either stops, or it arrives looking like it came from five different companies.

Does a system make everything look the same?

The opposite of the risk people expect. What makes work look generic is reaching for a template because there is nothing else to reach for. A system that is specific to you — your type, your image treatment, your way of framing a claim — is what makes the output recognisable at a glance and impossible to confuse with a competitor.

How much faster is production, really?

The gain is not in drawing faster. It is in deciding faster. Most of the time between a good idea and a published post goes on decisions that were already made once and are being remade from scratch. When those are settled, a post takes minutes and a report takes an afternoon.
