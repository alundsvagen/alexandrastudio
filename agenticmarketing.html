How, and where not to

Agentic Marketing: How?

Agents are very good at preparation and very bad at judgement. In a market where a few hundred people decide everything, that distinction is the whole strategy.

Read what we build

The honest version

Most of what is sold as agentic marketing is volume outreach with better grammar. It works briefly, in large markets, until recipients learn the pattern — and they learn it quickly. The Nordic fintech market is a few hundred people who talk to each other. Getting caught automating a relationship here costs more than the pipeline it generates.

The useful application is upstream, where nobody sees the output and the leverage is real.

Agents prepare. Humans decide.

Committee mapping at speed

Building a named map of six to ten decision-makers across thirty target accounts is a week of tedious work for a person. An agent working from public sources gets to a reviewable draft in an afternoon. A human checks it, because the errors are confident and plausible.

Pre-event briefs

Forty registrants, each with a company, a role and a reason for attending. An agent produces a briefing sheet before the event so your founder walks in knowing which four conversations matter. This is the single highest-return use we have found.

Signal monitoring

Regulatory changes, funding announcements, leadership moves at target accounts. Continuous, cheap, and genuinely better than a human doing it inconsistently.

First drafts from real source material

A recording of a talk becomes four post drafts. The material is yours; the transcription and structuring is not worth a person's afternoon.

The line we hold

Nothing reaches a named buyer without a human deciding it should. Not a message, not a comment, not a follow-up. In a regulated category with a small, well-connected audience, that is not caution — it is the only version that survives contact with the market.

Where agents earn their keep

Account research, committee mapping, pre-event briefs, signal monitoring, list hygiene, transcription and structuring, first drafts from your own material, and answer audits across assistants.

Where they cost you

Unsupervised outreach, automated comments, invented case studies, personalisation at volume, anything touching regulated claims, and any output published without a named person accountable for it.

Questions people ask before the call

What is agentic marketing?

Marketing work carried out by AI agents that can take multi-step actions on their own — researching accounts, drafting sequences, updating records, monitoring signals — rather than a person prompting a model for one output at a time. The useful version keeps a human accountable for anything a customer will see.

Where do agents genuinely help a small fintech team?

Research and preparation, mostly. Building a buying-committee map from public sources, monitoring regulatory changes, preparing pre-event briefs on registrants, cleaning and deduplicating lists, and drafting first versions from source material the team produced. All of it is work a small team simply does not have hours for.

Where should agents stay out?

Anything that reaches a named buyer unsupervised. Automated personalised outreach at volume is now instantly recognisable, and in a market of a few hundred people, being recognised as automated is a durable reputational cost. In regulated categories it can also be a compliance problem.

Do you use AI in client work?

Yes, for research, drafting and analysis, and it is disclosed. Nothing goes out under a client's name without a human deciding it should. The rule is simple: agents can prepare, humans decide.
