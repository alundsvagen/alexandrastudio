How to use executive roundtables for B2B pipeline development

A roundtable can be the best hour your sales team spends all quarter, or an expensive breakfast. The difference is almost entirely in the design. Here is how we build them, from the rooms we've run for fintech and B2B companies in Stockholm.

By Alexandra Grisanti Published 25 October 2026 10 min read

The short answer

An executive roundtable creates pipeline when it is designed backwards from the accounts you need: named people from those accounts, invited with a co-host they already trust, around one question they genuinely want answered, with the discussion led by peers rather than your sales team.

Follow up within 48 hours, track meetings booked within 30 days, and run the next room for the same accounts. Measure verified attendance, target-account coverage and buying-committee depth before you measure anything else.

Why roundtables work on senior buyers

Senior buyers are hard to reach because everyone is trying to reach them. They ignore ads, delete cold email and walk past booths. But they do clear a morning for a well-chosen room: a small table of peers, one question they care about, and no one selling at them.

  • Two hours, not ten minutes. At a conference, you get a few minutes with people who are often there to sell too. Around one table, you get a real conversation with the people you invited.
  • In person still wins. Research in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology found a face-to-face request was 34 times more likely to get a yes than the same request by email (Harvard Business Review).
  • The whole committee can come. A fintech deal often needs six to ten people to agree. A roundtable for CFOs, then one for heads of product, reaches the people your champion has to convince.
  • Peers are the draw. People rarely feel natural networking. They do want to learn from people who carry the same weight they do, and they want to be seen doing it. A seat in the discussion is a stronger invitation than a seat in the audience.

When a roundtable is the wrong tool

Be honest about this before you book a venue. A roundtable is probably not right if:

  • You can't name the accounts you want in the room. Without a list, you'll fill seats with whoever is available.
  • You need volume. A roundtable gives you depth with 12–20 people, not reach to 2,000.
  • Nobody on your side will follow up. Without follow-up, it's hospitality.
  • You're pre-launch and have nothing to discuss yet but your product.

Designing a roundtable for pipeline

1. Start from the account list

Name 30–50 target accounts and the roles you need at each. The guest list is built from that list, not from whoever replies first. Every seat should map to an account you'd be glad to report on.

2. Pick one sharp question

The topic is the invitation. It should be something the role recognises immediately and rarely discusses openly. Real examples from rooms we've designed:

  • Is growth a judgement call? A CEO + CFO breakfast, led by Fairlo's Head of Finance and Gilion's CFO & Deputy CEO.
  • Trust is your product, and also your constraint. A CMO breakfast with Mindler and Natural Cycles.
  • Enduring speed while building trust. A CMO breakfast with Legora.

Notice none of them is about the host's product. That's deliberate.

3. Size it for conversation

About 8–12 for a dinner and 12–20 for a breakfast. Invite more than you need, because some confirmed guests will drop out, and set a go/no-go date: about three weeks out, look at the confirmed list. If it's not the room you wanted, move the date rather than run a thin event.

4. Bring a co-host your buyers trust

A co-host turns your invitation from a sales approach into a peer event. It can be a bank, a partner, or an adjacent company that sells to the same people. Choose for local credibility, not size. How to choose and work with partners.

5. Let peers lead the discussion

Two or three discussion leads from outside your company: customers, partners, respected practitioners. Ask each to prepare a short summary of what they're seeing in their role and three questions they genuinely want the room to answer. Share any reading in advance.

6. Invite personally

Named invitations from a person, not a campaign. Send them through your network, your co-host's and any community you're part of. Consider a small commitment step, such as a short application or a nominal fee; one CFO breakfast we ran used a 100 kr fee as a signal that registered guests intend to come.

7. Say clearly who is hosting

Your company should be named as host on the invitation, with the co-host beside you. Guests should never wonder who invited them, and you should never be introduced as a "sponsor" of your own room.

The follow-up is the pipeline

The room creates the relationship; the follow-up creates the opportunity. Within 48 hours, every guest should get something useful from the host: a short summary of the discussion (without attribution, if you ran it under the Chatham House rule), the reading, and one specific next step that fits what they said in the room. Then:

  • Your champion follows up personally with the target accounts that came.
  • Someone covers the accounts that didn't come, with the content from the room.
  • Meetings booked in the next 30 days are logged against the event.
  • Every guest is invited to the next room.

How to measure it

Attendance is where measurement starts, not where it ends. We report every roundtable series against the same scorecard:

For reference: the CEO + CFO breakfast we produced for Cardboard in September 2026 had 18 approved registrations and 15 people checked in on the day. That's the kind of number to report first.

Why a series beats a one-off

A single roundtable is a good morning. A series is a relationship. When the same accounts are invited back, the second conversation starts where the first ended, and guests begin to bring colleagues, which is how you reach the rest of the buying committee.

Our first series was three CMO breakfasts for the Stockholm agency Heijbel Growth. It gave the agency repeat, peer-level access to the CMOs it sells to, and it brought two new prospects, Natural Cycles and Mindler, into the room as speakers rather than as cold approaches. That pattern, invite people onto the stage before you ever pitch them, is one of the most reliable we know.

Questions people ask

How many people should attend an executive roundtable?

Small enough for everyone to speak: about 8 to 12 for a dinner and 12 to 20 for a breakfast. Invite more than you need, because some confirmed guests drop out, and set a go/no-go date so you can move the event if the confirmed list is too short.

Should the host company present its product at a roundtable?

No. A short welcome that says who is hosting and why the topic matters is enough. The discussion should be led by peers, such as customers, partners or respected practitioners. Senior guests come for the conversation, and a pitch is the fastest way to make sure they don't come back.

How do you turn a roundtable into pipeline?

Design it backwards from the accounts you need, invite named people from those accounts with a co-host they trust, and follow up within 48 hours with something useful, such as a summary of the discussion and a specific next step. Then track meetings booked within 30 days, and run the next room for the same accounts so relationships deepen.

How do you measure the ROI of an executive roundtable?

Start with counted leading indicators: verified attendance, target-account coverage, buying-committee depth, qualified conversations and meetings booked within 30 days. Then track pipeline, stage progression and revenue in your CRM. Keep counts and estimates separate, and label any pipeline value as an estimate with its assumptions.

Is a roundtable better than a conference booth?

They do different jobs. A booth buys presence and brief conversations with people who are often also selling. A roundtable buys an hour or two around one table with the people you invited. Many companies combine them by hosting a dinner or breakfast beside a conference they already attend.
